# Nicotine — Topic-by-Topic Notes

> **Source:** *Nicotine - Wikipedia (1).pdf* supplied in this conversation.  
> **Level:** Simple explanations suitable for a 9th-standard student.  
> **Important:** Research claims are described as research findings, not as proven medical treatments. Regulatory information in the source is time-sensitive.

## 1. What is Nicotine?

- Nicotine is an **alkaloid** (a nitrogen-containing natural chemical) and a **stimulant** (a substance that increases activity of the nervous system).
- It occurs mainly in plants of the **nightshade family (Solanaceae)**, especially tobacco.
- Tobacco leaves contain about **0.6–3% nicotine by dry weight** in the introductory section of the source.
- Small amounts occur in tomatoes, potatoes, eggplants and other Solanaceae plants.
- Pure nicotine is a colourless to yellowish, oily liquid.
- It can pass through biological membranes and acts as a powerful **neurotoxin** (a substance that can damage or disrupt nerve function), especially in insects.
- In humans, nicotine mainly acts by activating **nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (nAChRs)**.

**Source:** PDF, pp. 1–2. fileciteturn5file0L12-L50

## 2. Main Effects in Humans

Nicotine activates nAChRs and changes the release of several **neurotransmitters** (chemical messengers between nerve cells), including:

- dopamine
- acetylcholine
- norepinephrine

Possible effects include:

- increased alertness
- reduced anxiety
- mild euphoria (a feeling of pleasure or well-being)

Nicotine is highly addictive. Dependence can involve:

- tolerance (needing repeated exposure for the same effect)
- physical dependence
- psychological dependence
- withdrawal symptoms such as irritability, anxiety and difficulty concentrating.

**Source:** PDF, p. 1. fileciteturn5file0L25-L50

## 3. Forms and Routes of Use

The source lists nicotine use through:

- cigarettes and other tobacco products
- electronic cigarettes
- nicotine pouches
- chewing tobacco
- cigars
- snuff
- snus
- pipe tobacco

Nicotine can be administered by inhalation, oral/buccal/sublingual routes, transdermally (through the skin), and other medical routes listed in the source.

**Source:** PDF, pp. 3–4. fileciteturn5file0L120-L129 fileciteturn5file0L237-L261

## 4. Medical Use — Nicotine Replacement Therapy (NRT)

**NRT** means nicotine replacement therapy. It supplies nicotine without tobacco smoke.

Examples:

- nicotine patches
- gum
- lozenges
- inhalers
- nasal sprays

The source explains that NRT delivers nicotine more slowly and at controlled doses than many recreational nicotine products.

A 2018 Cochrane review cited by the source found that NRT increased successful smoking cessation by about **50–60%**.

Using a nicotine patch together with a faster-acting NRT such as gum or spray was reported to improve treatment success.

**Important:** NRT is intended to help people stop smoking; this does not mean nicotine itself is harmless.

**Source:** PDF, pp. 2–3. fileciteturn5file0L55-L86

## 5. Nicotine as an Insecticide

Nicotine has been used as an insecticide since at least **1690**.

It acts on insect nicotinic acetylcholine receptors and can kill insects.

Nicotine insecticides were later replaced to a large extent by other insecticides that were cheaper and less harmful to mammals.

The source says nicotine insecticides were banned in the US in 2014 and in the EU in 2009.

**Source:** PDF, pp. 2–3. fileciteturn5file0L88-L95 fileciteturn5file0L132-L167

## 6. Neonicotinoids

**Neonicotinoids** are synthetic insecticides that are chemically related to nicotine.

Example mentioned in the source:

- imidacloprid

Unlike traditional surface-applied nicotine pesticides, neonicotinoids are **systemic**: the plant absorbs them and they spread through plant tissues.

**Source:** PDF, p. 3. fileciteturn5file0L157-L167

## 7. Nicotine and Cognitive Performance

The source discusses research in which nicotine-containing products were studied for effects on cognition.

A 2010 meta-analysis of 41 double-blind, placebo-controlled studies reported positive effects on some:

- fine motor abilities
- alerting and orienting attention
- episodic memory
- working memory

A later review linked some attentional effects to stimulation of the **α4β2 nicotinic receptor**.

These findings should not be interpreted as a recommendation to use nicotine for studying.

**Source:** PDF, p. 3. fileciteturn5file0L169-L182

## 8. Pharmacokinetics

**Pharmacokinetics** means what the body does to a chemical: absorption, distribution, metabolism and removal.

The source gives:

- Inhalation bioavailability: **80–90%**
- Intranasal: **60–80%**
- Transdermal: **68–100%**
- Protein binding: **<5%**
- Main metabolism: liver
- Important enzymes: **CYP2A6, CYP2B6 and FMO3**
- Major metabolite: **cotinine**
- Nicotine half-life: about **1–2 hours**
- Cotinine half-life: roughly **16–20 hours** in the source

**Source:** PDF, pp. 3–4 and 15. fileciteturn5file0L212-L225 fileciteturn5file0L755-L784

## 9. Nicotine Receptors

Nicotine acts as an **agonist** (a substance that activates a receptor) at most nicotinic acetylcholine receptors.

The source states that it acts as an **antagonist** (blocks receptor activity) at α9 and α10 receptor subunits.

Nicotine has particularly high affinity for the **α4β2 receptor**, which is strongly involved in nicotine's addictive effects.

**Source:** PDF, pp. 4 and 11. fileciteturn5file0L228-L235 fileciteturn5file0L651-L666

## 10. Central Nervous System (CNS)

The **CNS** consists mainly of the brain and spinal cord.

Nicotine:

1. enters the bloodstream;
2. reaches the brain quickly after inhalation;
3. binds to nAChRs;
4. changes neurotransmitter release.

It particularly affects the **mesolimbic dopamine pathway**, a brain reward pathway.

Nicotine activates receptors involving the **ventral tegmental area (VTA)** and can increase dopamine release.

This reward-related activity contributes to reinforcement and addiction.

**Source:** PDF, pp. 12–13. fileciteturn5file0L669-L701

## 11. Acute and High-Dose Effects

Nicotine can show different effects at different doses.

At lower or typical doses it can stimulate the nervous system. At very high doses it can reduce neuronal activity and can become dangerous.

The source describes a shift from stimulation toward sedation at increasing doses, sometimes called **Nesbitt's paradox**.

Very high exposure can produce:

- muscle effects
- breathing problems
- seizures
- respiratory paralysis

**Source:** PDF, p. 12. fileciteturn5file0L669-L688

## 12. Long-Term Brain Changes

Chronic nicotine exposure can cause changes in the brain, including:

- receptor desensitization (receptors temporarily become less responsive)
- increased numbers of some nAChRs (**upregulation**)
- changes in dopamine-related systems
- changes in gene-regulating proteins such as **ΔFosB**
- changes in reward pathways

The source describes some recovery after stopping nicotine, but the time varies depending on the biological measure and study.

**Source:** PDF, pp. 13–14. fileciteturn5file0L703-L737

## 13. Sympathetic Nervous System

Nicotine activates the **sympathetic nervous system**, part of the body's rapid-response system.

It stimulates the adrenal medulla to release:

- epinephrine (adrenaline)
- norepinephrine

This can increase:

- heart rate
- blood pressure
- breathing
- blood glucose
- force of heart contraction

**Source:** PDF, p. 14. fileciteturn5file0L739-L754

## 14. Cardiovascular System

Nicotine can:

- increase heart rate
- increase blood pressure
- increase the workload of the heart
- cause blood-vessel narrowing (**vasoconstriction**)
- affect coronary blood flow
- affect endothelial function

The source distinguishes nicotine's effects from the much broader toxicity of cigarette smoke, which contains substances such as carbon monoxide and oxidant gases.

A Cochrane review cited in the PDF found that NRT did not increase serious cardiac events compared with controls, although chest pain and palpitations can occur rarely.

**Source:** PDF, pp. 6–7. fileciteturn5file0L410-L446

## 15. Addiction and Reinforcement

Nicotine is highly addictive.

Its addictive potential depends partly on:

- how quickly nicotine reaches the brain
- how it is administered
- the form of nicotine
- other components of tobacco smoke

Withdrawal can include:

- anxiety
- irritability
- craving
- low mood
- reduced pleasure (**anhedonia**)
- mild physical symptoms

The source says withdrawal symptoms generally peak within **1–3 days** and can continue for several weeks.

**Source:** PDF, pp. 7–8. fileciteturn5file0L447-L480

## 16. Nicotine, Smoking and Cognitive Function

In dependent smokers, smoking can temporarily reverse some attention and memory problems caused by nicotine withdrawal.

Therefore, an apparent improvement after smoking does not necessarily mean that nicotine makes overall daily cognitive ability higher than that of non-smokers.

The source says overall daily cognitive levels were roughly similar in the cited research.

**Source:** PDF, pp. 7–8. fileciteturn5file0L468-L498

## 17. Cancer

A key distinction:

- The source states that **nicotine itself does not cause cancer in humans**.
- However, it discusses evidence that nicotine may affect biological processes involved in tumour growth and metastasis.
- Nicotine can activate signalling pathways involving nAChRs, including α7 receptors.
- The source discusses processes such as cell proliferation, angiogenesis (new blood-vessel formation), migration and invasion.

The source also discusses the formation of carcinogenic tobacco-specific nitrosamines during tobacco processing and possible nitrosation reactions.

Therefore:

> **“Nicotine is not classified as a carcinogen” does NOT mean nicotine is harmless.**

**Source:** PDF, pp. 8–9. fileciteturn5file0L515-L550

## 18. Pregnancy and Breastfeeding

The source describes nicotine as a possible **teratogen** (a substance that can interfere with normal fetal development).

Nicotine:

- crosses the placenta
- can enter breast milk
- has been associated with developmental effects in animal studies
- is associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes in smoking populations

The source notes that the exact effects of nicotine alone in humans are difficult to separate from other tobacco exposures.

**Important:** The PDF states that nicotine is safer than smoking in the context of NRT, but it is not considered completely safe during pregnancy or breastfeeding.

**Source:** PDF, pp. 5 and 9. fileciteturn5file0L263-L276 fileciteturn5file0L552-L573

## 19. Nicotine Poisoning / Overdose

Very high nicotine exposure can cause poisoning.

Early symptoms may include:

- nausea
- vomiting
- diarrhoea
- excessive saliva
- abdominal pain
- fast heart rate
- high blood pressure
- rapid breathing
- headache
- dizziness
- sweating
- pale skin

More severe poisoning can cause:

- slow heart rate
- slow breathing
- low blood pressure
- drowsiness
- confusion
- fainting
- weakness
- seizures
- coma
- respiratory paralysis
- death

Children can be affected by lower doses per kilogram than adults.

The PDF specifically states that the **median lethal dose in humans is unknown**, so exact “lethal dose” claims should not be treated as a safe threshold.

**Source:** PDF, pp. 9–10. fileciteturn5file0L575-L608

## 20. Green Tobacco Sickness

**Green Tobacco Sickness (GTS)** is a form of nicotine poisoning caused by absorption of nicotine through the skin from wet tobacco leaves.

It can occur in people who harvest or cultivate tobacco, particularly inexperienced workers who do not normally use tobacco.

**Source:** PDF, p. 10. fileciteturn5file0L610-L625

## 21. Drug Interactions

Nicotine and cigarette smoking can affect medicines.

The source discusses possible interactions with:

- sympathomimetic drugs
- sympatholytic drugs
- several medicines metabolized by liver enzymes

Importantly, **stopping smoking can change drug metabolism**, because cigarette smoke—not simply nicotine—can induce certain liver enzymes.

The source lists examples including caffeine, theophylline, some beta-blockers and tricyclic antidepressants.

**Source:** PDF, pp. 10–11. fileciteturn5file0L627-L650

## 22. Nicotine and Sleep

Nicotine can reduce:

- REM sleep
- slow-wave sleep
- total sleep time

It can also increase the time needed to fall asleep and change sleep-stage timing.

Nicotine withdrawal can negatively affect mood and sleep.

**Source:** PDF, p. 6. fileciteturn5file0L400-L408

## 23. Pharmacology and Stereochemistry

Nicotine is **chiral**, meaning its molecule can exist in two mirror-image forms called enantiomers:

- **(S)-nicotine**
- **(R)-nicotine**

Natural tobacco nicotine is overwhelmingly the S form.

The source states that S-nicotine is substantially more active than R-nicotine in standard receptor and functional tests.

**Source:** PDF, p. 11 and p. 17. fileciteturn5file0L651-L666 fileciteturn5file0L870-L895

## 24. Chemical Properties

### Molecular formula
**C₁₀H₁₄N₂**

### Molar mass
**162.236 g/mol**

### Other properties
- chiral compound
- oily liquid
- hygroscopic (absorbs moisture)
- colourless to pale yellow
- can become brown when exposed to air or light
- volatile
- characteristic strong odour
- basic nitrogen-containing compound

The source gives:
- melting point: about **−79 °C**
- boiling point: about **247 °C**
- density: about **1.01 g/cm³**

**Source:** PDF, pp. 5 and 16. fileciteturn5file0L370-L381 fileciteturn5file0L830-L857

## 25. pH, Ionization and Nicotine Salts

Nicotine has two nitrogen atoms that can participate in protonation.

Its pKa values given in the source are approximately:

- **8.10** for the pyrrolidine nitrogen
- **3.41** for the pyridine nitrogen

At physiological pH (~7.4), nicotine is mostly **monoprotonated (positively charged)**.

Above pH 8, more nicotine becomes the unprotonated **free-base** form.

### Nicotine salts
Acids can be added to nicotine to form protonated nicotine salts.

The source discusses salts such as:

- nicotine benzoate
- nicotine lactate
- nicotine levulinate

The source reports research showing that some nicotine-salt aerosols can produce high nicotine delivery while feeling smoother in the throat than free-base nicotine.

**Source:** PDF, pp. 15–16. fileciteturn5file0L786-L814

## 26. Nicotine and Body Weight

The source reports that nicotine can:

- decrease hunger
- decrease food consumption
- increase energy expenditure

Research generally shows weight reduction in the studied contexts, although some animal studies have reported different effects under particular eating conditions.

The source discusses α3β4 nAChRs, POMC neurons and the melanocortin system as part of the proposed mechanism.

**Important:** This is not a recommendation to use nicotine for weight control.

**Source:** PDF, p. 16. fileciteturn5file0L816-L828

## 27. Synthesis of Nicotine

The first laboratory preparation of nicotine as a racemic mixture was reported in **1904**.

Many synthetic methods have since been developed.

**Racemic** means a mixture containing both mirror-image forms, generally in approximately equal amounts.

**Source:** PDF, pp. 17–18. fileciteturn5file0L902-L911

## 28. Biosynthesis in Tobacco

Nicotine is produced naturally by tobacco plants.

Its two major structural portions come from different biological pathways:

1. The **pyridine ring** comes from nicotinic acid-related metabolism.
2. The **pyrrolidine portion** comes from an N-methyl-pyrrolidium intermediate.

The source describes pathways involving:

- NAD pathway
- tropane pathway
- aspartate oxidase
- quinolinate synthase
- QPT
- ornithine decarboxylase
- putrescine N-methyltransferase
- N-methyl putrescine oxidase

The final coupling mechanism is described as not being completely established.

**Source:** PDF, pp. 18–19. fileciteturn5file0L913-L954

## 29. Detection in Body Fluids

Nicotine can be measured in:

- blood
- plasma
- urine

**Cotinine** is often measured because it remains in the body longer than nicotine.

Cotinine measurements may be used in some medical, occupational and research settings.

Passive exposure to tobacco smoke can also produce nicotine metabolites, so test results require careful interpretation.

**Source:** PDF, p. 19. fileciteturn5file0L956-L979

## 30. Natural Occurrence

Nicotine is a **secondary metabolite** (a chemical produced by a plant that is not directly part of basic growth).

The source gives tobacco concentrations varying by species and context:

- *Nicotiana tabacum*: about **0.5–7.5%**
- *Nicotiana rustica*: about **2–14%**

Nicotine production increases after plant injury through a jasmonate-dependent response.

Some insects that feed on tobacco, such as the tobacco hornworm (*Manduca sexta*), have adaptations that help them tolerate or use nicotine.

**Source:** PDF, p. 20. fileciteturn5file0L999-L1011

## 31. Nicotine in Vegetables

Very small amounts of nicotine can occur in:

- potatoes
- tomatoes
- eggplant
- peppers

The source gives approximately **2–7 μg/kg** in some Solanaceae plants.

Tomato nicotine levels decrease substantially as the fruit ripens.

The source cites estimated dietary nicotine intake of roughly **1.4–2.25 μg/day at the 95th percentile**, while noting uncertainty because the amounts are extremely small and difficult to measure accurately.

**Source:** PDF, pp. 20–21. fileciteturn6file0L11-L29

## 32. Biological Function in Tobacco

For tobacco, nicotine acts mainly as an **antiherbivore defence**.

It is a neurotoxin for many insects.

Nicotine is produced in tobacco roots and transported to leaves.

Some specialist insects have evolved resistance to it.

**Source:** PDF, p. 20. fileciteturn6file0L31-L38

## 33. History of Nicotine

Key dates in the source:

- **1828:** nicotine isolated from tobacco by Wilhelm Heinrich Posselt and Karl Ludwig Reimann.
- **1843:** empirical formula described by Melsens.
- **1893:** structure discovered by Adolf Pinner and Richard Wolffenstein.
- **1904:** first laboratory synthesis by Amé Pictet and A. Rotschy.

The name ultimately comes from *Nicotiana tabacum* and the name of Jean Nicot de Villemain.

Tobacco reached Europe by the early 1530s through Spanish explorers.

**Source:** PDF, pp. 21–22. fileciteturn6file0L41-L73

## 34. Tobacco and Historical Trade

The source describes tobacco becoming popular in Europe because of its stimulating effects and its historical reputation as a medicinal “cure-all.”

In the early 17th century, tobacco became an important cash crop in Virginia.

The source specifically discusses John Rolfe's introduction of tobacco cultivation in 1612 and more than 20,000 pounds being shipped by 1619.

**Source:** PDF, p. 21. fileciteturn6file0L63-L73

## 35. Synthetic Nicotine

Laboratory methods for producing synthetic nicotine have existed for decades.

Historically, synthesis was considered too expensive compared with extracting nicotine from tobacco.

The source notes that synthetic nicotine later appeared in some:

- e-cigarettes
- oral nicotine pouches

and was marketed as “tobacco-free.”

**Source:** PDF, p. 22. fileciteturn6file0L85-L91

## 36. Regulation

### United States
The source states that the FDA regulates nicotine-containing tobacco products under the 2009 tobacco-control law.

In 2022, federal legislation expanded FDA tobacco authority to products containing nicotine from **any source**, including synthetic nicotine.

The source says that on January 17, 2025, the FDA proposed a rule intended to reduce nicotine in cigarettes and certain combusted tobacco products to minimally or non-addictive levels.

**Important:** The PDF describes this as a **proposal**, not as an automatically implemented rule.

### European Union
The **Tobacco Products Directive 2014/40/EU** regulates tobacco and related products.

### United Kingdom
The source describes the Tobacco and Related Products Regulations 2016 and later amendments.

**Source:** PDF, pp. 22–23. fileciteturn6file0L93-L118

## 37. Age Limits Mentioned in the PDF

The source states:

- United States: federal tobacco purchase age **21**
- US OTC NRT: **18+**
- EU: source states **18**, except Latvia **20**
- UK tobacco purchase: **18**

Because laws can change, these figures should be checked against the current law before being used for legal decisions.

**Source:** PDF, p. 23. fileciteturn6file0L119-L130

## 38. Nicotine in Media and Advertising

The source discusses public misunderstanding of nicotine's biological effects.

It also describes **Nick O'Teen**, an anti-smoking character created for the Health Education Council and used in animated public-service announcements.

The source says nicotine was compared with caffeine in some tobacco-industry advertising in the 1980s and later in electronic-cigarette advertising in the 2010s.

**Source:** PDF, p. 23. fileciteturn6file0L132-L148

## 39. Research: Depression

The source describes research in which chronic low-dose nicotine caused receptor desensitization and reported antidepressant effects.

It mentions early research suggesting that low-dose nicotine patches might be effective for major depressive disorder in non-smokers and says antidepressant effects have also been reported in mice.

**Important:** These are research findings cited by the PDF, not evidence that nicotine should be used as a depression treatment.

**Source:** PDF, pp. 23–24. fileciteturn6file0L150-L163

## 40. Research: Alzheimer's Disease

The source states that smoking is associated with increased Alzheimer's disease risk while also describing evidence suggesting that nicotine itself may have potential effects relevant to Alzheimer's disease.

These statements concern research and should not be interpreted as proof that nicotine prevents or treats Alzheimer's disease.

**Source:** PDF, p. 24. fileciteturn6file0L164-L168

## 41. Research: Parkinson's Disease

The source states that smoking is associated with a lower risk of Parkinson's disease and discusses nicotine's possible neuroprotective effects.

It also describes possible dopamine-related effects and reports an association involving left medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) atrophy in smokers who develop Parkinson's disease.

The source notes that **reverse causation** and non-nicotine components of cigarette smoke may contribute.

**Important:** This does not establish nicotine as a Parkinson's treatment.

**Source:** PDF, p. 24. fileciteturn6file0L169-L176

## 42. Research: Schizophrenia

The source reports that nicotine may partly improve sensory-gating and attention-related deficits.

Short-term transdermal nicotine improved reaction time and alertness in certain tasks.

However, the source says nicotine did **not** improve positive symptoms, negative symptoms, or other cognitive symptoms of schizophrenia in the cited research.

**Source:** PDF, p. 24. fileciteturn6file0L177-L180

## 43. Brain Networks in Heavy Smokers

The source discusses research suggesting altered brain-network organization in heavy smokers.

Some altered network measurements were correlated with:

- duration of cigarette use
- severity of nicotine dependence

This describes an association reported in research; it does not by itself prove a simple cause-and-effect relationship.

**Source:** PDF, p. 24. fileciteturn6file0L182-L185

## 44. Possible Long-Term Effects on the Medial Habenula

The source discusses a proposed mechanism involving the **medial habenula (mHb)** and its connections with the interpeduncular nucleus.

It reports that nicotine exposure might damage or kill some neurons involved in nicotine-avoidance behaviour, but explicitly says that more research is needed.

Therefore, the statement that all long-term nicotine effects are irreversible would be incorrect.

**Source:** PDF, p. 24. fileciteturn6file0L187-L190

## 45. 2026 “False Productivity” Claim

The PDF contains a claim attributed to a 2026 *Nature* study saying that nicotine can mimic acetylcholine in the nucleus accumbens, trigger dopamine release and affect how behaviours are assigned value.

The PDF uses the terms **“salience dysregulation”** and **“false productivity.”**

### Important verification note
This is a specific research claim in the supplied PDF. It should be treated as a **reported research interpretation**, not as a universal fact about every nicotine user.

**Source:** PDF, p. 24. fileciteturn6file0L192-L195

## 46. Immune System

Immune cells can contain several nicotinic acetylcholine receptor subunits, including:

- α2
- α5
- α6
- α7
- α9
- α10

The source reports that nicotine's immune effects are mostly anti-inflammatory in the studies discussed, although some effects can be pro-inflammatory.

Reported effects include reduced:

- IL-1
- IL-6
- TNF-α
- IL-17
- IL-21
- IL-22

The source also describes reduced T-cell activity and dendritic-cell activation.

**Important:** These findings do not mean nicotine should be used as an immune treatment.

**Source:** PDF, pp. 24–25. fileciteturn6file0L197-L224

## 47. Nicotine and Cancer/Immune Function

The source states that nicotine's effects on immune function may contribute to tumour growth and metastasis in cancer patients.

At the same time, some experimental research has reported potentially useful effects in autoimmune diseases such as:

- inflammatory bowel disease
- ulcerative colitis
- arthritis

The source says further research is required.

**Source:** PDF, p. 25. fileciteturn6file0L210-L224

## 48. Optopharmacology

**Optopharmacology** is the use of light-controlled chemicals to study biological processes.

The source describes a photoactivatable form of nicotine that can release nicotine under ultraviolet light under specific conditions.

It was developed to study nicotinic acetylcholine receptors in brain tissue.

**Source:** PDF, p. 25. fileciteturn6file0L226-L230

## 49. Oral Health

The source reports that nicotine can have pro-inflammatory effects in oral diseases.

It discusses possible worsening of:

- periodontitis
- gingivitis

especially when harmful microorganisms are present in the mouth.

However, the source explicitly says that available data are insufficient, particularly **in vivo** (inside living organisms).

**Source:** PDF, p. 25. fileciteturn6file0L231-L238

## 53. Quick Facts

- **Formula:** C₁₀H₁₄N₂
- **Molar mass:** 162.236 g/mol
- **Major metabolite:** cotinine
- **Main metabolic enzyme:** CYP2A6
- **Nicotine half-life:** about 1–2 hours
- **Cotinine half-life:** about 18–20 hours in the pharmacokinetic section
- **Main addictive receptor discussed:** α4β2 nAChR
- **Natural tobacco form:** predominantly (S)-nicotine
- **First isolation:** 1828
- **Structure discovered:** 1893
- **First laboratory synthesis:** 1904
- **Nicotine in tobacco:** roughly 0.6–3% in the introductory description; another section gives 0.5–7.5% depending on tobacco context/species
- **Nicotine in some vegetables:** only trace amounts
- **NRT examples:** patch, gum, lozenge, inhaler, nasal spray

## 54. Most Important Things to Remember

1. **Nicotine is addictive.**
2. **Nicotine is not the same thing as tobacco smoke.**
3. Nicotine itself is **not classified as a human carcinogen** in the source, but it is not harmless.
4. Tobacco smoke contains many other toxic and carcinogenic chemicals.
5. Nicotine activates **nicotinic acetylcholine receptors**.
6. Nicotine strongly affects the brain's **dopamine reward system**.
7. High exposure can cause **nicotine poisoning** and can be fatal.
8. Nicotine is particularly concerning during **pregnancy and adolescence**.
9. **NRT is used to help people quit smoking** and avoids the toxic products of tobacco combustion.
10. Many neurological, immune and cancer-related findings in the source are **research findings or hypotheses**, not established treatments.
